The paper under Kael's boots had a voice. It wasn't loud. It was the scratch of a pencil behind the world, and it said, “19:03. The Gate opens. The actors are late.”

Mira grabbed his wrist. The floor had stopped being tile a moment ago; now it was cardstock, thick and faintly warm, with blue guide lines printed underfoot: STAGE LEFT / CENTER / STAGE RIGHT. The station's arrival board had changed from electric orange to black ink on white paper, and every row read the same impossible announcement: “NEXT ACT: 19:03 — ENTER TWO PANEL HOLDERS.” The central Gate no longer looked like a cracked tear. It had become a proscenium arch, complete with gold-paper molding, side curtains, and a dark mouth inside that hummed with the sound of a page being turned.

Kael's hand went to the hilt of his sword. “It's not a Gate anymore.”

“It's a stage,” Mira said. Her Panel was open at her wrist, scrolling with notifications she didn't read. “Kael, look.”

The script page in his jacket pocket was warm. He pulled it out. The long paper strip had printed a new line across its blank face in perfect type:

ENTER: TWO PANEL HOLDERS. TIME: 19:03. THE GATE HAS A MOUTH. IT REQUIRES AN ACT.

His Panel buzzed. Slot Zero shimmered at the bottom of his vision, and the words refreshed:

VERSION 1.0: SCRIPT PAGE LOADED — STAGE MANAGER CUE

“Did you get that?” Mira asked.

“Don't read it aloud.” Kael tried to shove the page back into his pocket.

Too late.

A voice emerged from behind them, too flat to be human but too polite to be a machine: “Welcome to the performance. I hope you've memorized your opening positions.”

The Stage Manager stood where the old information kiosk had been. It was the color of newsprint, folded into a long-limbed figure in a black paper suit. Its face was blank except for a printed smile, slightly crooked, and around its neck hung a placard: DO NOT READ THE CUE LINES. It carried a conductor's baton made from a rolled-up train schedule.

“Kael and Mira,” the Stage Manager said. “Act One of this Gate was serviceable. The Stagehand underperformed, but that is what understudies are for. The current script requires a hero's sacrifice.” It tapped the baton against its palm. “You will surrender the Rift Core and the Script Page. Then the Gate will close, the city will be declared stable, and both of you will live to appear in Act Three as supporting cast.”

Kael drew his sword. “What if we don't want the part?”

“Then I will mark you as uncooperative. Uncooperative characters are rewritten.”

The floor rippled.

Kael moved first. The skill branch First Strike fired in his arms—a tight, explosive diagonal from shoulder to waist, meant to end a fight in one breath. The blade sheared through the air and then through the paper suit. But the suit folded sideways at the waist like a crease in a map, and the sword passed through the gap without resistance.

“Before your cue,” the Stage Manager said, “you are not real.”

It tapped the baton. A wave of paper rolled across the floor, curled around Kael's ankles, and threw him against the red curtain rail. The curtain was stiff as plywood. He hit, slid down, and heard Mira shout.

She was already moving. A curtain had become a sheet of wallpaper, sweeping toward her in a wide fold. Mira grabbed a fallen station sign and swung it like a bat. The sign slammed into the Stage Manager's shoulder, tearing its suit and revealing blank white pages underneath. It didn't bleed.

“Unpolished line reading, Mira. We'll fix it in rehearsal.”

The baton came down. Kael lunged across the paper floor, tackled Mira aside, and the baton struck the cardstock, opening a black crack in the stage. Cold air breathed out of it, smelling of ink and old books.

The Stage Manager raised its baton again. The benches in the station flickered, and paper cutouts of commuters appeared in the seats. They had no faces, no hands—only folded white shapes printed with the suggestion of coats and hats. They began to clap. A dry rustle filled the room, and the Stage Manager smiled wider.

“Every performance needs an audience,” it said.

The cutout commuters stood. All at once. They shuffled toward Kael and Mira, arms outstretched, their blank heads tilted as if listening for applause.

Mira kicked the nearest one. It tore in half, but two more took its place. Kael cut through three of them in a single First Strike, and the shredded paper scattered across the floor like confetti. More kept coming.

“Okay,” Mira said, breathing hard. “It knew we were going to move before we moved.”

“It knows our lines,” Kael said. “The Stage Manager is reading the script. That means our actions are predictable.”

“Then we need to do something that isn't in the script.”

Kael pulled out the script page. Its line hadn't changed: THE GATE HAS A MOUTH. IT REQUIRES AN ACT. The page had reacted to Slot Zero before. Maybe Slot Zero was the only thing the script didn't cover.

He held up the page. “Stage Manager. Read this one.”

The Stage Manager's smile rotated into a frown. “The Script Page belongs to the Gate. It is part of my stage. Put it down.”

“Come and take it.”

The Stage Manager moved differently this time. It did not fold; it rushed, covering the stage in four long strides, baton raised. Its printed smile was gone, replaced by an empty white mask. Kael waited until the baton was almost at his face. Then he didn't swing.

He opened Slot Zero.

Not a skill, not an item. He summoned the extra slot in his Panel and poured everything he knew into it: the version line, the Gate location, the impossible word that had been burning in his vision since the System update. VERSION 1.0. GATE OPEN. The script page flared in his hand, white and hot, and the words slammed through the paper and into the Stage Manager's chest.

“Un—authorized—” the Stage Manager said. “VERSION WORD MISMATCH.”

It froze. Its paper body shuddered, and lines of script began to race across its suit like water soaking into tissue.

The rift core in Kael's pack pulsed, hot. He pulled it out. The core was alive with light, and the script page reached for it, curling around the faceted stone on its own. The page and the core became one object: a paper-wrapped sun.

Kael drove it into the Stage Manager's chest.

The Stage Manager didn't scream. It folded, layer by layer, its arms and legs creasing inward, its printed face collapsing into the shape of a star, then a dot, then a blank sheet. The paper stage held its breath. A single word stamped itself across the folded pile: EXIT.

The Gate's proscenium arch crumbled. Gold trim fell like confetti, the red curtains tore into scraps of paper, and the dark mouth of the Gate snapped shut with a soft, final thump. The station lights blinked back on. The floor was tile again. The clocks were still on paper, but they jumped from 7:03 to 7:04, as if time had decided to keep running.

The paper cutout commuters sagged and flattened into ordinary receipts scattered across the waiting area.

Kael picked up the rift core and the small script-page stub from the pile. The core was cold. The stub was warm. A single line of text had survived the burn, still dark and legible:

THE ZERO SLOT HAS ALREADY CHOSEN THE NEXT GATE.

Mira exhaled. “You just used Slot Zero as a weapon.”

“It used itself.” Kael tucked the stub beside the core. His Panel buzzed.

Slot Zero refreshed:

VERSION 1.0: ACT II CUE — NEXT GATE IS A DOOR MADE OF SIGNS

“That's not a place,” Mira said.

“Maybe it's literal.” Kael pointed at the station's departure board. The orange letters were back, but every row flickered to the same time: 7:03, 7:03, 7:03. The sign above the main doors read CENTRAL STATION in clean white letters. It looked ordinary. It also looked like a door that was waiting.

The Warden's voice cut through the room, flat and clipped:

“UNAUTHORIZED GATE EVENT CONTAINED. THE STAGE MANAGER WAS NOT IN THE SYSTEM SPECIFICATION. NO XP REWARD GENERATED. NO ITEM DROP GENERATED. VERBAL ACKNOWLEDGMENT REQUIRED.”

Kael raised his head. “Acknowledged. But I didn't write that stage.”

“CAUSATION IS IRRELEVANT. CONTAINMENT IS THE METRIC. THE NEXT EVENT WILL REQUIRE CLEARANCE. RETAIN THE RIFT CORE. RETAIN THE SCRIPT PAGE. EXPECT A DOOR.”

The voice vanished.

Mira let out a long breath. “It knew the Gate was here.”

“It knew something.” Kael slid the script stub into his jacket. “The Stage Manager was waiting for version 1.0. The Warden says it wasn't in the spec. Which means someone built that stage before the System released it.”

“Who?”

“The same person who put Slot Zero in my panel.”

They didn't talk much after that. The station's cleanup crew would arrive eventually, but until then, the building was theirs. They walked through the paper debris and into the café that Guild Alpha used as a meeting room.

The booth smelled like stale coffee and cheap ink. A map of August City covered the table, held down by mugs. Someone had drawn a red circle around Central Station and written beneath it: “Gate moved. Scouting sewer mouths. Don't die.” The third member of the guild was already gone.

Kael unfolded the map fully. He placed the rift core in the center. The core sat still for a moment, then began to turn slowly, its facets catching the light. When it stopped, one small facet aligned not with the sewer mouth on the map but with the printed word “SEWER” in the map's legend.

“That's not pointing at the place,” Mira said.

“It's pointing at the word.” Kael tapped the legend. “The clue said a door made of signs. That's a sign.”

The script-page stub twitched. Kael unrolled it and read the line that had been hiding beneath the first:

THE VERSION WORD OPENS ALL DOORS. THE ZERO SLOT IS THE VERSION WORD.

He stared at the words. Slot Zero had never been a bug. It was a key. And every time he used it, the key turned something inside the world—something that wasn't supposed to move until version 1.0 opened it.

Mira sat across from him, chin resting on her hands. “We didn't get XP.”

“We didn't get a drop.”

“We got a clue.” She looked at the core and the stub. “And a gate that's actually closed.”

“For now.” Kael turned the rift core over. It was cool to the touch, but he could feel a faint pulse under the facets, like the heartbeat of a machine waiting to be watched.

The café clock finally moved to 7:05.

The Gate was closed. The stage was empty. But the paper had made one thing clear: the performance wasn't over.
