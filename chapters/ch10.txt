Kael did not move when the glass doors hissed shut behind them. Mira caught his arm. “Kael. We’re out.”

He kept staring upward. The metal sign over the north exit had not changed: AUGUST CENTRAL STATION — NORTH, in the same brushed-steel letters that had been there all day. But the letters looked too arranged, too polished, as if someone had dusted them for a performance. Through the window, the station’s main departures board clattered through an update. The orange characters read 14:07 PLATFORM 7. Then, for half a second, they read 14:07 DOOR 7. Then they were back to PLATFORM 7.

“Did you see that?” Kael asked.

Mira followed his gaze. “The departures board? It’s just a schedule.”

“It said door.”

He opened his Panel. The blue window unrolled in front of him, and the line in Slot Zero was still there: VERSION 1.0: ACT II CUE — NEXT GATE IS A DOOR MADE OF SIGNS. He read it aloud. Mira checked her own notifications; nothing appeared.

“It’s the same clue,” she said. “The station sign, the board—maybe they’re all just pointing at one thing.”

Kael took out the script-page. It was creased from the old theater, the ink faint but neat. At the top of the sheet, someone had typed ACT II, and below it:

CUE: NEXT GATE IS A DOOR MADE OF SIGNS.
A sign can lie. A door cannot.
The first key opens. The second quiet answers.

“That’s the whole page now?” Mira leaned over his shoulder. “It did something different before.”

“Maybe I wasn’t ready to see the rest.” Kael folded it carefully. “A sign can lie. A door cannot. So we’re not looking for a normal door. We’re looking for a sign that pretends it isn’t a door.”

Mira stepped under the north sign and looked up. “There’s paper taped to the underside.” She pulled down a white card. The handwriting matched the script-page. It said only: A sign can lie. A door cannot.

“Same message,” Kael said. “It’s a confirmation.”

They walked around the plaza. A café had a hand-lettered menu board: TODAY’S SPECIAL: NOTHING. A bus shelter carried a single line: QUIET ZONE. Kael stopped at the bus stop. “Quiet zone. That’s a sign about silence.”

Mira looked at the empty bench. “First quiet?”

“Maybe.” He filed it away. The riddle had named a second quiet, not a first.

Two blocks east, a service alley gaped between a bookshop and an insurance office. Kael stopped at the mouth. A red EXIT sign hung above a boarded-up door. The door had rusted hinges and posters layered over the frame. The sign was clean, freshly screwed into the brick. It said EXIT in bright red letters, with a small white arrow pointing down at the boards.

“There,” Kael said. “That exit doesn’t exit. It’s a lie.”

Mira frowned. “A lie, but is it a door?”

“The clue says a door made of signs. The sign is the door. Not the wall behind it.”

He walked closer. As he reached toward the sign, the EXIT letters flickered once, as if the power had wobbled. The air around them changed, suddenly too still.

“You’re early.”

Kael spun. The man in the overcoat stood by a dumpster, hands in his pockets, collar high. He had been behind them the whole time, or maybe not behind them at all. He smiled like a stagehand who knew the play by heart.

“Early is close enough,” the man said. “The crew changed the departures board nine times before they got the message to hold. You still walked out the north door. So that makes you either the right one or the second one.”

“Second?” Kael said.

“The second quiet,” the man said, as if that explained everything.

Mira moved closer to Kael. “Who are you?”

“I’m the one the Warden says isn’t on the schedule.” He tipped his head. “That has always been true. It doesn’t mean I don’t exist.”

Kael studied him. “You’re the Stage Manager.”

The man’s smile widened. “You’ve been reading the wrong lines in the right order.”

“What do you want?” Mira asked.

“Not me. The door.” He gestured at the EXIT sign. “You have the first key. The core from the sewer mouth. You have the cue. You have not yet found the second quiet. That’s why it’s still closed.”

Kael’s hand moved toward his jacket, where the rift-core sat inside a sealed pouch. The sign pulsed again, a deeper red.

A rectangle of light opened in front of Kael, cold and grey, not his usual Panel. The Warden’s voice did not come from the streets or the speakers. It came from inside the window, word by word:

KAEL. THE STAGE MANAGER IS NOT IN THE SYSTEM BUILD. IF THE ACT II GATE OPENS ON HIS CUE, THE CITY RIFT COUNTER WILL ARM. THERE WILL BE NO RETURN TO ACT I WITHOUT CLEARANCE. DO YOU ACCEPT?

The words hung in the air. Mira read them from Kael’s face. “Kael, no.”

“It’s a warning,” Kael said. “Not a quest.”

The man in the overcoat laughed softly. “The Warden sends warnings because he can’t send help. The counter will arm. The stage will move. That’s what Act II does.”

Kael placed his palm on the EXIT sign. It was warmer than the air. The red glow deepened. The letters shifted under his fingers. E-X-I-T folded apart like a set of doors, and behind them the wall was not a wall anymore. A narrow corridor opened, lined with old theatre sign panels: arrows, neon letters, doorways with words above them. The air smelled of dust and ozone. At the far end, a faint gold light pulsed.

“That’s the next gate,” Mira breathed.

“It’s a door made of signs,” Kael said.

The man in the overcoat did not step closer. “If you walk through without the second quiet, you’ll be no better than the audience. You’ll see what the play wants, and not the thing behind it.”

Mira caught Kael’s sleeve. “Then we don’t walk through yet.”

Kael looked at the script-page again. The line still waited. The second quiet was still missing.

The corridor shivered. The EXIT letters folded back one by one, becoming flat metal. The gold light went out. The alley returned: dumpster, boards, posters, and a red sign that lied.

“The performance is waiting for the second quiet,” the man said.

He turned and walked away, overcoat flapping.

Mira let out a breath. “That was a dead end.”

“No. It was a cue.” Kael shut his Panel. “We know the gate. Now we need the quiet.”

They walked back toward the main street, under the ordinary signs, while the city kept its routines and the Panels waited for someone to say the right unsaid thing.
