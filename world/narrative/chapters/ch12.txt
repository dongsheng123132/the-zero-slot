The door clicked shut behind them, and the sound was gone.

Kael stood in a long low corridor that smelled of dust, old paper, and the particular dry heat of a theater before the lights come up. The walls weren't walls anymore. They were signs.

Not the neat green-and-white kind that told you which exit to use in a fire. These were older, rougher. Plywood rectangles, bedsheets stiffened with paint, torn cardboard, one brushed-aluminum street sign that had mostly lost its letters. They overlapped like leaves fallen against a chain-link fence, nailed and stapled and pasted one on top of another.

The nearest said:

THE FIRST QUIET IS THE LIE.
WELCOME TO IT.

Mira read it, then turned to Kael. "The first quiet is a lie?"

"I think the second quiet is the one that matters." Kael had been holding that phrase in his head for a full day — second-quiet — ever since the Warden passed it along. "That's part of the cue. 'Say the word, then be still.' The word is quiet. So the first quiet is the silence before you say it, and the second quiet is the silence after."

"That doesn't sound like a door."

"It's not. It's how the door opens."

They walked.

The signs on the walls changed as they passed. Not visibly, not all at once — Kael would glance at a sign, look away, look back, and the words would be different.

SAY NOTHING YOU MEAN.
MEAN NOTHING YOU SAY.

And then, when he looked twice:

THE STAGE MANAGER RESPECTS ONLY PROPS THAT ARE LOADED.

"Kael." Mira stopped. She pointed at the floor.

A line had been painted across the concrete — black, about an inch wide. Stenciled letters, small and clean, read: C7.

Kael's Panel flickered. A notification settled into the corner of his vision: GATE DOOR C7 UNDER THE EXIT SIGN. "This is it. The line marks the route."

"To what?"

"To the next gate." He stepped over the line, and the sign on the wall beside him switched from a faded lost-dog notice to a single sheet of white paper with bold black type:

ACT II.
MIND THE ARMS COUNTER.
DO NOT BRING OTHERS' ARMS.

Past the line, the corridor widened. The sign-pile on the left rose like a snowdrift, and half-buried in it was something that did not belong — the slumped back of a man in a long overcoat.

Kael froze. "Hey," he said.

The man didn't turn. He was digging through the pile, muttering, tossing aside sheets of laminate and cardboard. Kael took a step closer, and the man straightened, holding a single metal sign in both hands — a yellow diamond, the kind used on roadsides. The front faced away.

The man turned.

The overcoat was familiar — dark wool, the coat he and Mira had glimpsed at the edge of the first rift, the man who had folded himself into the shadows outside the central station. The face was still hidden under the brim of a wide hat. The man looked at Kael for one heartbeat. Then he placed the yellow sign on top of the pile, face-down, and walked away.

"Wait," Kael said.

The man didn't stop. He opened a door Kael had not seen — a thin black door set flush in the wall, no handle, no sign above it — and stepped through. The door closed with a click. The wall was a wall again.

Mira reached the pile first. She flipped the yellow sign over. On the front, in heavy lettering:

ARMED MEANS MORE THAN TWO.

Kael read it over her shoulder. "He left us a warning."

"Or a lesson." Mira looked up the corridor. "What did the Warden say? 'Unscheduled entry, arms counter.' It counts arms, not weapons, right?"

"Right." Kael took the sign from her and propped it against the wall. "So if armed means more than two, we count as two each. The counter should be fine with us."

"Fine. Then why leave us a warning?"

"Because we're not the only ones in this corridor."

They moved on.

The corridor opened into a round room, like the base of a silo. In the center stood a waist-high gray metal podium. On it sat an old-fashioned counting machine — a mechanical counter whose red digits read 0. A sign bolted to the podium said:

ARMS COUNTER
ONE PER PERSON.
EXCEPT ACTORS. ACTORS GET PROPS.

Mira circled it. "It's just a counter."

"Warden said it's active." Kael stepped up to it. "Maybe we have to present ourselves. One at a time."

"Present our arms?"

"I don't know. The sign says one per person. We're two people. Two arms each." He pressed the button on the counter. It clicked. Display: 1.

Then the counter clicked again on its own. Display: 2.

Mira raised her eyebrows. "I didn't touch it."

It clicked. Display: 3.

Kael stepped back. "Mira — behind the podium."

Click. Display: 4.

A sound came from the tunnel behind them. A wet footstep. Then another, like boots half full of mud. Slow. Heavy.

Kael and Mira dropped flat behind the podium.

The shadow moved across the sign-covered walls — long, distorted, sliding, and it had too many arms. Kael counted six. Then eight. Nine. The arms waved independently, like tree branches in a wind that wasn't there, each one holding a sign. A red octagon. A blue arrow. A laminated fire notice. The wet steps came into the room.

Click. Display: 5.

The shadow stopped at the edge of the room. Kael saw a sliver of the thing in the gaps between piles: a body like a bundle of wet coats, each of its many arms holding a different sign, swaying, as if trying to decide where to post them.

Click. Display: 6.

The thing circled the podium. The signs in its hands knocked together — tip-tap, tip-tap — and the creak of its coat was the creak of old wood under weight. Then it stopped directly beside the podium, and the counter did not click again.

The Warden's message surfaced in Kael's peripheral vision, a small cold type:

THE COUNTER REQUIRES AN EVEN NUMBER BEFORE THE GATE WILL ACKNOWLEDGE A PASSAGE. IT DOES NOT COUNT ITSELF.

Six. The number was even. The thing stood there, arms gently swaying, six arms, six signs, six even numbers.

Then the wet footsteps resumed — moving away, down the corridor on the far side of the room. The shadow shrank, and the sound faded into silence.

Kael let out a breath he had been holding. "It was posting signs. It didn't care about us."

Mira stood, wiping dust from her palms. "It just... walked through. It had six arms. The counter counted six and let it pass."

"Because even counts are legal here. That's the rule." Kael looked at the counter, now resting at 6. "The Warden warned me about this. Unscheduled entry. Arms counter. I thought it would attack us. It only counts."

"You said 'only.'"

"The only thing it counts is arms. And it has to be even." He turned toward the corridor ahead. "We're two people. Two arms each. We can pass."

Together they walked past the podium. The counter clicked once as Kael crossed — 7 — and once more as Mira crossed — 8. No alarm. The red digits glowed behind them, pleased and quiet.

The corridor narrowed, then opened again. And at the far end, under a glowing red exit sign, stood a door.

But it was not a door of wood or metal.

It was made of signs.

Signboards of every kind — plywood, aluminum, plastic, canvas, corrugated cardboard — stacked so tightly they formed a single rigid leaf, their corners nailed through each other, their surfaces facing outward. It filled the space beneath the exit sign perfectly, from floor to frame. No handle. No hinges. No doorframe. And every single sign bore the same word, in sixteen different styles:

QUIET.

Kael counted them. Painted, printed, stenciled, carved, burned, spray-painted, embossed. QUIET. QUIET. QUIET.

"A word door," Mira said softly.

"That's the gate." Kael's voice felt louder than it should have been in the corridor. "A door made of signs. Slot zero was right."

"How do we open it?"

Kael looked at the signs. "The Warden's riddle. 'Second quiet means say the word, then be still.' The first quiet is the silence now. The word is 'quiet.' Saying it is the cue. Then we go still. That's the second quiet."

"Say 'quiet' and stay quiet?"

"Yes." He stepped closer. A small blank plywood sign, about the size of a paperback, lay at the foot of the door — one of the QUIET signs had come loose and fallen. Kael picked it up. The word was nearly faded, barely readable, but the grain of the wood held the ghost of it. He slipped it into his jacket pocket. It might be useful later. "Stay behind me."

He faced the door.

"Quiet," he said.

The word dropped into the corridor like a stone into deep water.

For a long moment, nothing. Then the lettering on the signs began to fade. Slowly, from the edges inward, each QUIET erased itself — not paint dissolving, but the idea of the word pulling back into the wood. Kael did not move. He did not breathe. He was the second quiet. Mira stood frozen beside him.

When the last letter of the last QUIET had faded to nothing, the door split down the center. One hairline crack that grew into an archway as the signboards peeled apart like pages of a heavy book.

Beyond was a stage.

Kael saw it in a single pulse of time: red velvet curtains, a black stage floor, a single spotlight burning in the dark, its beam cutting through dust like the road of a god. No actors. No audience. Just the theater, waiting.

And the Warden's voice — not far, not distorted, but close, soft, almost inside the archway:

"Unregistered actors enter Stage Two." A pause. "The Stage Manager is not pleased. But the audience is curious."

Kael opened his mouth, and the Warden continued, quieter:

"The first quiet opened the door. The second quiet will close it. Learn what the Stage Manager's voice does to the word."

The words settled into Kael's chest like a second heartbeat. The door made of signs was not just an entrance. It was a device, and the word was loaded.

"Kael." Mira's hand on his elbow. "Behind us."

He turned. The corridor was still there. The sign piles had shifted; the man in the overcoat was gone. And on the wall directly behind them, where only blank plywood had been, a new sign had appeared:

THE STAGE MANAGER HAS THE QUIET WORD TOO. HE WILL SAY IT WITH A BOOM.

Kael read it twice. Then he turned back to the archway.

"Stay close," he told Mira. "And be quiet."

"Always," she said.

They stepped through. The signboards closed behind them with a sound like pages closing. The corridor and its arms counter and piles of signs were gone. In their place: the overwhelming smell of dust and velvet, and the quiet before a performance.

In the center of the stage sat a wooden stool. On it, a script bound in red cloth, with a green exit-sign placard taped to its cover. Kael's Panel updated: ACT II — THE QUIET WORD. He picked up the script. The first page read:

"VERSION 1.0: ACT II CUE — THE WORD 'QUIET' IS A DOOR. CLOSE IT WHEN THE STAGE MANAGER SPEAKS. DO NOT LET HIM SAY IT."

Below, in handwriting he didn't recognize: "Miss the cue, and the arms counter will remember you."

His Panel pulsed. A soft chime rolled through him — LEVEL UP. He was level 5. Five unspent points waited in the corner of his vision. He dismissed the notification.

"Kael." Mira looked at the script in his hands. "What now?"

Above them, in the darkness of the rigging, something heavy shifted. A voice like gravel dropped into the silence.

"Page twenty-six. Say it with a boom."

The spotlight went red.
